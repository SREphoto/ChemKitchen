# Oxidize - Chemistry Lab Operation

## Purpose
To cause a substance to lose electrons (increase oxidation state) through reaction with an oxidizing agent. Oxidation is fundamental to redox chemistry, organic synthesis (alcohol→aldehyde→acid), combustion, corrosion studies, and analytical chemistry.

## How It Works
An oxidizing agent (oxidizer) accepts electrons from the substrate (reductant). In organic chemistry, this typically involves increasing oxygen content or decreasing hydrogen content. Common oxidizers include O₂, H₂O₂, KMnO₄, CrO₃/H₂SO₄, NaOCl, and HNO₃. The reaction may require catalyst, heat, or controlled pH.

## Setup
- Fume hood (oxidizers may produce toxic or corrosive fumes)
- Reaction vessel (round-bottom flask, beaker, or pressure reactor)
- Stirrer (magnetic or overhead)
- Temperature control (ice bath for exothermic, heating for activation)
- Addition funnel for slow oxidizer addition
- pH monitoring (pH meter or indicator paper) if aqueous
- Inert gas (N₂/Ar) if air-sensitive substrate

## Usage
1. Dissolve substrate in appropriate solvent
2. Cool reaction if highly exothermic (many oxidations generate heat)
3. Add oxidizing agent slowly with stirring - rate controls exotherm
4. Monitor reaction progress via TLC, GC, or color change
5. Control temperature to prevent over-oxidation or decomposition
6. When complete: quench excess oxidizer (with reducing agent like Na₂S₂O₃ or NaHSO₃)
7. Extract, wash, dry, and purify product

## Results
Product identification (NMR, IR, MS) and yield calculation. Oxidation state change confirmed by spectroscopy or chemical tests.

## Completion
Reaction complete when TLC/GC shows no starting material or when characteristic color of oxidizer disappears (e.g., purple KMnO₄ → brown MnO₂).

- Borosilicate glass (reaction vessels - most oxidizers are corrosive)

## Power
- Hot plate/stirrer: 100-500W
- Overhead stirrer: 50-500W
- Basic oxidation: no electrical power needed (manual setup)

## Cost
- Basic oxidation setup: $200-$500
- Reagent oxidizers: $20-$500 per reagent (KMnO₄ cheap, OsO₄ expensive)

## Models
- Chemical oxidation (using reagent oxidizers like KMnO₄, CrO₃, NaOCl)
- Electrochemical oxidation (using applied potential)
- Catalytic oxidation (using O₂ with catalyst - Pd/C, Pt, Cu)
- Enzymatic oxidation (using oxidoreductases)
- Photocatalytic oxidation (using light + TiO₂)

## Display / Memory / USB / Connectivity / Voice / Buttons
Standard for manual oxidation: none (manual glassware). Automated systems may have LCD, USB, Ethernet as per synthesis equipment.

## Ventilation
Required. Oxidations can release toxic fumes (NOₓ from HNO₃, Cl₂ from NaOCl, Cr vapors from CrO₃). Use fume hood.

## Limitations
- Over-oxidation is common (e.g., aldehyde→carboxylic acid when alcohol target)
- Many strong oxidizers are hazardous (explosive, corrosive)
- Some oxidizers are expensive or heavy metal-based (toxic waste)
- Selectivity can be poor without careful condition control

## Cleaning
- Quench and rinse glassware immediately (oxidizer residues can be hazardous)
- Remove Cr residues with dilute HCl or ascorbic acid
- Remove MnO₂ residues with dilute HCl or NaHSO₃
- Standard lab detergent wash after decontamination

## Storage
- Store oxidizers separately from reducing agents
- Store in cool, dry place away from organic materials
- H₂O₂: in vented containers, dark, cool
- Solid oxidizers (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇): in tightly sealed containers

## Disposal
- Heavy metal oxidizers (Cr, Mn, Os): special hazardous waste disposal
- Quench before disposal: reduce with NaHSO₃ or Na₂S₂O₃
- Follow institutional hazardous waste protocols
- Never mix oxidizers with organic waste

## Safety
- Highly exothermic - add oxidizer slowly with cooling
- May produce toxic gases; always use fume hood
- Contact with organic materials may cause fire
- Wear acid/oxidizer-resistant gloves (not just nitrile for strong oxidizers)
- Have spill kit and fire extinguisher available
- Never add water to strong oxidizer concentrates
- Know incompatibilities (e.g., KMnO₄ + glycerin = fire)
